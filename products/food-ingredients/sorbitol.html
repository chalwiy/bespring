Product identity

What is food grade sorbitol?

Sorbitol, also called D-glucitol, is a polyol used in foods as a sweetener, humectant, bulking agent, texturizer, stabilizer and sequestrant. The FAO/WHO JECFA monograph identifies crystalline sorbitol as INS 420(i), CAS 50-70-4.

Sorbitol syrup is a separate food-additive identity, INS 420(ii). It is an aqueous polyol preparation whose non-sorbitol fraction can vary with the feedstock and process. Buyers therefore need the exact form, composition basis and source-specific specification—not only the word “sorbitol.”

The decisive purchasing choice

Crystalline sorbitol vs. sorbitol syrup

Search results often combine “sorbitol powder,” “sorbitol 70% solution” and “non-crystallizing sorbitol,” but these terms describe materially different handling and formulation questions.

E420(i) / INS 420(i)

Crystalline sorbitol

Choose a dry form when water addition is undesirable or when dry blending, particle behavior and dissolution are important.

  • Confirm D-sorbitol and total glycitols on an anhydrous basis
  • Specify powder, crystal, granule or required particle-size range
  • Evaluate flow, caking, dust, dissolution and blend segregation
  • Control moisture because crystalline sorbitol is hygroscopic
E420(ii) / INS 420(ii)

Sorbitol syrup

Choose an aqueous form when liquid handling and pre-dissolved solids fit the process. “70%” commonly refers to total dry substance, not automatically 70% pure D-sorbitol in the finished liquid.

  • Confirm total solids and sorbitol on the stated dry or as-is basis
  • State crystallizing or non-crystallizing requirement where relevant
  • Review mannitol, reducing sugars and other polyols/saccharides
  • Evaluate viscosity, pumping, temperature and microbiological controls

Availability boundary: this page does not assert that every form or composition is always available. Bespring sales must match the offered source and current signed specification to the RFQ.

Function-led application screening

Where sorbitol performance must be tested

Sorbitol contributes more than sweetness. Changing form or replacing sucrose by weight can alter water, total solids, texture, freezing behavior and process conditions.

Chewing gum and confectionery

Screen bulk, sweetness, cooling sensation, moisture retention and crystallization behavior. Test chew or bite, surface condition, stickiness and stability in the finished product.

Bakery, fillings and icings

Evaluate softness and moisture retention alongside dough/batter water, bake loss, browning, filling migration, stickiness and shelf texture. Syrup water must be included in the formulation balance.

Frozen and chilled desserts

Assess freezing-point response, solids, sweetness, overrun, ice-crystal development and melt behavior. Validate with the full sugar, stabilizer and milk-solids system.

Liquid foods and preparations

Check clarity, viscosity, taste, pH compatibility and microbial control. Confirm whether the selected syrup profile remains stable at storage and processing temperatures.

Claims are formulation- and market-specificThe presence of sorbitol alone does not substantiate “sugar-free,” “reduced sugar,” “low calorie,” dental or diabetes-related claims. Review the complete formulation, serving basis, label and destination-market rules.

Formulation decision

Do not treat sorbitol as a one-to-one sucrose replacement

A technically sound trial separates sweetness from bulk, water management, texture and freezing behavior.

  1. 1

    Define the job

    State the target sweetness, water activity/moisture, solids, texture, shelf response and process constraints.

  2. 2

    Select the form

    Account for the water in syrup or the dissolution and particle behavior of crystalline material.

  3. 3

    Rebalance the system

    Adjust water and other sweeteners, bulking agents or stabilizers using measured rather than assumed equivalence.

  4. 4

    Validate over shelf life

    Measure sensory profile, texture, crystallization, migration, microbial stability and packaging interaction under intended conditions.

Storage and handling

Protect the selected sorbitol form

  • Crystalline materialKeep approved packaging tightly closed and protect hygroscopic material from moisture, contamination and damage.
  • SyrupFollow source instructions for storage temperature, pumping and hygiene; viscosity and crystallization behavior can be temperature-sensitive.
  • Receiving controlsMatch product name, form, lot, label, seal, COA and shelf-life status before releasing material.

Buyer questions

Food grade sorbitol FAQ

Is sorbitol powder the same as sorbitol 70% solution?

No. Crystalline sorbitol is a dry E420(i) ingredient. Sorbitol syrup is an aqueous E420(ii) polyol preparation. A “70%” syrup description usually concerns dry substance and must not be read as an automatic statement of D-sorbitol purity.

What is the difference between E420(i) and E420(ii)?

E420(i) identifies sorbitol as the crystalline substance; E420(ii) identifies sorbitol syrup. The syrup may contain other hydrogenated saccharides/polyols, so its full composition and crystallizing behavior require separate confirmation.

Which sorbitol form is better for confectionery or bakery?

There is no universal answer. Powder avoids adding syrup water and makes particle properties relevant; syrup is already dissolved but changes the water and liquid-handling balance. Test moisture, texture, crystallization, stickiness and shelf performance in the complete formulation.

Can a sorbitol ingredient page support a sugar-free or diabetes claim?

No. Such claims depend on the complete product, serving/composition criteria and the law of the destination market. Bespring does not use a single ingredient as proof of a finished-food health or nutrition claim.

What should a sorbitol RFQ include?

State crystalline E420(i) or syrup E420(ii), intended application, solids and composition basis, crystallizing requirement if relevant, critical test limits, quantity, packing, destination, Incoterm and required documents.
